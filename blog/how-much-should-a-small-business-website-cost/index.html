How Much Should a Small Business Website Actually Cost?

Ask five different people this question and you'll get five different answers: free, a few hundred dollars, a few thousand, tens of thousands. None of them are wrong, they're just answering about completely different things. Here's what actually drives the number, so you can tell whether a quote is reasonable before you say yes to it.

Why the range is so wide

"A website" isn't one product. A single-page site for a local business and a custom e-commerce store with inventory management are both "a website," priced nothing alike. Most of the confusion comes from comparing quotes without comparing what's actually included.

What actually drives the price

Number of pages. A one-page site is faster to design, write, and build than a five or ten page site, and it shows in the price. If you don't need separate pages for every service, a well-organized single page can do the job for less.

Custom design vs. a template. A template gets you online fast and cheap, but so does everyone else using the same template. Custom design costs more because someone is designing specifically for your brand instead of filling in a pre-built layout.

Who writes the copy. Writing the actual words for your site (not just the code) takes real time. Some freelancers include it, most treat it as a separate add-on. If a quote seems unusually low, check whether copywriting is even in it.

Functionality beyond "informational." A contact form is simple. Online booking, e-commerce with payment processing, or user accounts are not, each of those adds real development time and typically real cost.

SEO setup. Basic on-page SEO (title tags, meta descriptions, proper headings) is cheap to include from the start. Retrofitting it onto a site that wasn't built with it in mind costs more later than it would have cost upfront. It's one of the cheapest things to get right early; see the 5 SEO mistakes I see most often for what "not built with it in mind" usually looks like.

Ongoing maintenance. A site isn't a one-time purchase if you want it to keep working: security updates, small content changes, occasional fixes. Some of that is a monthly fee, some is billed as-needed. Either way, it's worth asking about before launch, not after something breaks.

DIY builders vs. hiring someone

Wix, Squarespace, and similar builders are genuinely fine for some businesses: very simple sites, tight budgets, someone comfortable doing their own updates. They're not a lesser choice, just a different tradeoff. You're trading a lower price and more of your own time for less customization and less SEO control than a hand-built site typically offers.

Hiring someone makes more sense once your time is worth more than the cost difference, or once you need something a template genuinely can't do well: a specific look, non-standard functionality, or SEO that needs more control than a builder gives you.

Freelancer vs. agency

Roughly speaking: DIY builders run free to a few hundred dollars a year in subscription costs. Freelancers doing custom work typically fall somewhere between a few hundred and a few thousand dollars, depending on scope. Agencies usually start where freelancers top out and go up from there, you're paying for a team and more overhead, not necessarily a better result for a small business site.

None of these is universally "better." A freelancer usually means working directly with the person actually building your site, no account manager in between, which tends to mean faster turnaround and clearer communication for a project this size. Questions worth asking before you compare quotes

  • Does this include copywriting, or just design and build?
  • How many pages, specifically?
  • Is basic SEO included, or an add-on?
  • How many rounds of revisions are included?
  • What happens after launch, is there any support window?
  • Who owns the final files, domain, and hosting?

Two quotes that look far apart often include completely different things once you ask these. That's usually the real explanation, not that one person is overcharging and the other is underselling themselves.

The number on a quote means very little on its own. What it includes is the actual comparison.

The honest bottom line

There's no single right price for a small business website, but there is a right question: what am I actually getting for this. A freelancer who explains their pricing clearly, in writing, before any work starts, is doing you a favor either way, it makes it easy to compare and easy to trust.
